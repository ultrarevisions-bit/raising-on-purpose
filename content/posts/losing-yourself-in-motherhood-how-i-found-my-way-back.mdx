## The Moment I Realized I'd Disappeared

I was standing in my kitchen at 6pm, holding my son on my hip, and someone asked me what I liked to do for fun. I genuinely could not answer. My son was four months old at the time, and I realized I hadn't read a book, finished a shower uninterrupted, or had a thought that wasn't about feeding schedules in weeks.

That's losing yourself in motherhood. Nobody warns you it happens this fast, or this quietly. You don't wake up one day and decide to disappear. You just keep saying yes to the baby's needs until there's no room left for your own, and by month four, you can't even remember what you used to want.

## Nobody Tells You It Starts This Early

For me it started in the hospital, actually. A nurse called me "mom" instead of my name, and I remember thinking that felt wrong, like she had the wrong person. By week six, everyone called me mom. My husband, my mother-in-law, even my own mother sometimes slipped. My name just stopped coming up.

And I let it happen because I thought that's what good mothers did. I thought losing myself was the price of admission, the tradeoff for loving this baby as much as I did. Looking back, that belief cost me almost two years of feeling like a stranger in my own life.

## Why This Isn't Just "New Mom Tiredness"

There's a real difference between being exhausted and being gone. Exhausted is the 3am feeding. Gone is forgetting what your own laugh sounds like because you haven't had a reason to use it in weeks.

Researchers actually have a word for the identity shift that happens when you become a mother: matrescence. It's treated like puberty, a total psychological and physical transformation, except nobody prepares you for it the way they prepare you for puberty. You're just supposed to figure it out while running on four hours of broken sleep.

I think that's part of why it feels so disorienting. You're grieving a version of yourself while simultaneously being told to be grateful. Both things can be true. You can love your baby fiercely and still mourn the person you were before 2am feedings became your whole personality.

## What Actually Started Bringing Me Back

I wish I had a dramatic turning point to give you. I don't. It was small, almost embarrassingly small things, done consistently.

- I started listening to one podcast episode a week that had nothing to do with parenting, usually while washing bottles.
- I texted a friend something other than a baby photo, just to prove I still had thoughts outside the house.
- I asked my husband to take our son for one hour every Saturday morning, no questions, no guilt, just mine.
- I wrote three sentences a day in a notebook about anything except the baby.

None of these felt life-changing in the moment. But around month nine, I noticed I had opinions again. I disagreed with someone in a group chat just because I actually believed something, not because I was performing being okay.

## The Guilt That Tries to Talk You Out of It

Here's the part nobody says out loud. When you start taking pieces of yourself back, guilt shows up fast and loud. It tells you that wanting an hour alone means you're not grateful enough, that missing your old life means you love your baby less.

That's not true, and I wish someone had told me sooner. Wanting to feel like yourself again isn't a betrayal of motherhood. A toddler doesn't need a mother who has erased herself. He needs one who's actually present, not just physically in the room while mentally checked out from exhaustion and resentment.

My son is two and a half now, and the mom he gets today is a better one than the one he had at four months, specifically because I stopped disappearing.

## What I'd Tell Someone in the Thick of It

If you're in the newborn fog right now, feeling like you've lost yourself in motherhood, I won't tell you it gets easier on a specific timeline, because that's not honest. But I will tell you that the woman you were didn't die. She's just buried under laundry and feeding schedules, and she's waiting for you to dig her back out.

Start absurdly small. Reclaim one five-minute window a day that belongs only to you. Not to productivity, not to the baby, just to you. It won't feel like enough. Do it anyway.

You're allowed to be both: deeply in love with your baby, and actively working to find your way back to yourself. Those two things were never in competition, no matter what the guilt tries to tell you at midnight.

If this is where you are right now, you're not failing. You're just in the middle of the hardest, most disorienting transformation nobody prepared you for, and you will find your way back.
