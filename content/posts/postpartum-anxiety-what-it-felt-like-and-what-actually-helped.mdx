## The Night I Counted My Daughter's Breaths Until 4am

She was eleven days old. I had the baby monitor propped against my pillow, volume all the way up, and I was still getting out of bed every twenty minutes to put my hand on her chest. Not because she'd done anything wrong. She was fine. I just could not believe she was fine, and some part of my brain refused to accept it until I checked again.

That's postpartum anxiety, at least the version I had. Nobody warned me it could look like this. I'd heard about postpartum depression, the crying, the numbness, the not bonding with the baby. What I had felt almost like the opposite. I was hyper alert, hyper focused, running on a current of dread that never fully switched off. I loved my daughter fiercely and was also convinced, somewhere under my ribs, that something terrible was about to happen to her.

## What Postpartum Anxiety Actually Felt Like, Day to Day

It wasn't one big dramatic moment. It was smaller than that and somehow worse because of it.

I'd be nursing at 3am and my heart would start racing for no reason I could point to. My thoughts would jump to worst case scenarios: what if I fell asleep holding her, what if the car seat strap wasn't tight enough, what if that weird newborn breathing pattern meant something was actually wrong. I knew, logically, that most of these thoughts were unlikely. Knowing that did nothing to stop them.

I also couldn't hand her off. My husband would offer to take a night shift and I'd say yes and then lie awake anyway, listening through the wall. I checked the baby monitor app so often my phone screen time report looked like I had a second job. At my six week checkup I mentioned I was tired. I didn't mention the part where I'd researched infant CPR four times that week out of pure panic, not preparation.

That's the part that gets missed in most conversations about new motherhood. Postpartum anxiety doesn't always look like falling apart. Sometimes it looks like someone who seems almost too on top of things, because being vigilant is the only thing that quiets the fear, even for a minute.

## Why Nobody Catches It Right Away

A lot of postpartum anxiety gets written off as normal new parent worry, and some of it genuinely is. Every new parent checks on a sleeping newborn more than they need to. The line gets crossed when the worry starts running your life instead of just visiting.

For me, the tell was that it didn't fade as my daughter got older or as I got more experienced. By eight weeks I'd fed, bathed, and changed a thousand diapers. I knew what I was doing. The anxiety didn't care. It just found new things to attach to: her weight gain, a weird rash, whether daycare would be safe someday, a day that was still months away.

If you're a few weeks or months into this and the fear hasn't loosened its grip even though you objectively know more than you did at the start, that's worth paying attention to. Competence doesn't cure this kind of anxiety. It needs something else.

## What Actually Helped Me

I want to be straightforward here because I read a lot of soft, vague advice during this time and none of it moved the needle. These are the specific things that did.

**Naming it out loud to my doctor, using the actual words.** I stopped saying 'I'm just tired' and started saying 'I think I might have postpartum anxiety.' That sentence got me a real conversation instead of a pamphlet about rest.

**A short course of therapy with someone who specialized in postpartum mental health, not general anxiety.** The difference mattered. A postpartum specialist understood why I couldn't just 'relax' when someone else held the baby, and gave me tools built for that specific flavor of fear.

**Medication, for a while.** I resisted this for weeks because I was breastfeeding and scared of every possible risk. My doctor walked me through options that were well studied and considered safe, and within about three weeks I felt like the volume on my brain had been turned down from an 11 to a 6. That was enough to function again.

**One specific person who knew the real version of me, not the Instagram version.** My sister called every single day at the same time for a month, not to check on the baby but to check on me. I didn't have to perform okay-ness for her. That mattered more than I expected.

**Letting go of the night checks, on purpose, in small steps.** My therapist had me extend the time between monitor checks gradually: fifteen minutes, then thirty, then an hour. It felt impossible at first. By week three it felt almost boring, which honestly was the whole point.

## What I'd Tell Someone in the Middle of It Right Now

If you're reading this at midnight with your phone brightness turned down so you don't wake anyone, and you've checked on your baby six times in the last hour, I want to say this plainly: this is treatable, and you are not a bad mother for having it.

Postpartum anxiety thrives on secrecy. It gets smaller the moment you say it out loud to the right person, a doctor, a partner, a friend who won't flinch. You don't have to wait until it's unbearable to ask for help. Tired and scared is enough of a reason.

It took me about four months to feel like myself again, and I want to be honest that it wasn't a straight line. There were good weeks followed by a rough night that made me feel like I'd slid backward. That's normal too. Healing from this isn't tidy, but it does happen.

## If You Need a Place to Start

Talk to your OB, midwife, or your baby's pediatrician this week, not next month. Postpartum anxiety responds well to treatment, and you don't have to white-knuckle your way through the newborn stage hoping it fades on its own. It gets better. I'm living proof, and so are a lot of mothers who never say this part out loud.
