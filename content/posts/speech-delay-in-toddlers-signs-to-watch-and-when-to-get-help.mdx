## The Text I Got at 11pm From a Friend Who Was Spiraling

She wrote: 'He's 22 months and only says mama and ball. My mother in law keeps telling me Einstein didn't talk until he was four. Am I overreacting?'

Short answer: no, you're not overreacting, and that Einstein thing is a myth anyway. If you're googling speech delay toddler symptoms at midnight, you already know something feels off. Trust that instinct. Pediatricians will often say 'wait and see,' and sometimes that's fine. But sometimes it means a kid loses a full year of catch-up time because nobody wanted to be the one to push.

I'm not a speech pathologist. I'm someone who has watched three different friends go through this with wildly different outcomes, and the ones who moved fast had an easier road. Let's talk about what to actually watch for.

## What Typical Speech Looks Like, Roughly

Every kid is different, but there are real benchmarks worth knowing. By 12 months, most toddlers say at least one word with meaning, like 'dada' pointed at dad specifically. By 18 months, that usually grows to somewhere between 10 and 20 words.

By age 2, a lot of toddlers are combining two words, something like 'more juice' or 'mommy up.' By 3, strangers should be able to understand most of what your kid says, even if grandma still needs a translator sometimes.

Those are averages, not deadlines carved in stone. Some toddlers are quieter and catch up fast. But when a kid falls noticeably behind more than one of these markers, that's worth paying attention to instead of waiting it out indefinitely.

## Signs That Go Beyond 'He's Just a Late Talker'

Here's where it gets more specific, because 'late talker' gets used as a comfort blanket a lot. A true late talker usually still understands everything you say, points to request things, makes eye contact, and uses gestures to communicate even without words.

Watch for these instead:

- Your 18 month old doesn't point to show you things or follow simple instructions like 'bring me the cup'
- Your 2 year old has fewer than 50 words and isn't combining any of them
- Your 3 year old is still mostly unintelligible to people outside the immediate family
- Your toddler stopped using words they used to say, like if they said 'bye' at 14 months and don't anymore at 20 months
- There's no babbling variety, like it's always the same sound over and over with no changing consonants

That last one, losing skills they already had, is the one that should move you to act fastest. Regression is a different conversation than delay, and it deserves a quicker conversation with your pediatrician.

## Why 'He'll Grow Out of It' Isn't Always True

I get why people say this. Plenty of kids genuinely do catch up on their own with zero intervention. My own son barely talked at 20 months and was chatting in full sentences by 3.

But here's the thing nobody tells you: speech delay at 2 or 3 can be the first visible sign of something bigger, like hearing loss, apraxia, or a broader developmental difference. Sometimes it's just speech. Sometimes it's not, and the only way to know is to get it checked.

Waiting costs nothing if everything turns out fine. Waiting costs real time if it doesn't. Early intervention services for speech are most effective under age 3, so the window matters more than people realize.

## What to Actually Do This Week

Start with your pediatrician, but don't accept 'let's wait another six months' as the only answer if your gut says otherwise. You can ask directly for a referral to a speech language pathologist for an evaluation.

Every state in the US has a free early intervention program for kids under 3, and you don't need a doctor's referral to call them yourself. Look up 'early intervention' plus your state name and make the call today instead of next month.

Get a hearing test too. Fluid in the ears from chronic infections is a sneaky, common cause of speech delay that gets missed constantly, especially in toddlers who've had a lot of ear infections before age 2.

## What This Looked Like for My Friend

She called early intervention the next morning after that 11pm text. Her son got evaluated at 23 months, qualified for services, and started seeing a speech therapist twice a month at home.

By 2 and a half, he had around 200 words. By 3, you'd never know he'd been delayed at all. She still says the hardest part wasn't the therapy, it was ignoring her mother in law long enough to make the first call.

That's really the message here. You don't need to panic over every quiet toddler. But if your gut has been nagging at you for weeks, that nagging is data too, not just anxiety talking.

## A Quick Gut Check

If you're still unsure, ask yourself this: has anyone outside your house, a daycare teacher, a grandparent, a pediatrician, mentioned a concern about your toddler's talking? Outside observations catch things parents sometimes can't see because we're too close to it.

And if you've been quietly worried for more than a couple months, that's long enough. Make the call. The worst outcome is finding out everything's fine. That's not actually a bad outcome at all.

If you're in the thick of this right now, you're not failing your kid by googling at midnight. You're paying attention, and that's exactly what they need from you.
